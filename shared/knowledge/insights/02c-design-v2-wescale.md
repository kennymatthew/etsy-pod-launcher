## Design Research Method

**Starting point: Etsy competitor scraping** | **Check** (Etsy-specific tactic, but principle applies to any marketplace)

The process is explicit:
1. Go to Etsy, search "[niche] t-shirts"
2. Filter: Show filter → "Etsy's Best" → "Star Seller"
3. Screenshot 10–15 designs that show "in demand" or "bestseller" badges
4. Save to a folder named "Etsy inspiration designs" — NOT to copy, purely for pattern recognition

Demand signals they look for on Etsy:
- "X people bought this in the last 24 hours" (they multiply by 7 for weekly estimate — e.g., "27 bought in 24h = ~150/week")
- "Bestseller" tag
- "In X carts right now"
- "Etsy's pick" (hand-selected by Etsy style experts)

**What to look for across 10–15 designs:** | **Evergreen**
- Collect 30–50 winning designs for your niche to reveal patterns
- "What do 20+ of these designs have in common? Write down three patterns, stop researching, and then start creating."
- The 80/20 research action plan: "Set a 60-minute timer. Answer, what do 20 plus of these designs have in common? Write down three patterns, stop researching, and then start creating."
- Cap research at 60 minutes to avoid diminishing returns; saving 100+ designs leads to analysis paralysis

**Proprietary AI research step:** | **Their tool**
After Etsy scraping, all inspiration screenshots are uploaded into the "We Scale Design and Research GPT" (custom tool, not publicly available). They ask it to:
- "Analyze these designs for my [niche] print on demand t-shirt brand"
- "Give me a demographic breakdown of the [niche] niche"
- "Give me more design direction for my [niche] brand"

The tool returns: visual style patterns, demographics, psychographics, trending slogans, visual motifs, cross-niche opportunities, typography/color palette recommendations, and subniches. Output is copy-pasted and saved to a Google Doc in the brand folder.

**Cross-niche research:** | **Evergreen**
Explicitly look for what golfers (or your niche) also like — the research GPT surfaced: "golf and beer/wine," "golf and dad/retirement," "golf and travel," "golf and humor about bad shots." These become design direction: "[Niche] x [complementary interest]" prompts.

**The core mindset shift:** | **Evergreen**
"It's not about the design itself. It's not about how good it looks, and it's not about picking the right colors. It's about what the market already wants."

## What Makes a Winning Design

**The single most repeated criterion: negative space.** | **Evergreen**

Described across almost every good design example: "We don't want just a giant block of print on our t-shirt, whereas we can use that negative space to create compelling effects."

Specific technical point: "If you're looking at the cat, specifically the black portions of the cat, there is actually no ink in the t-shirt there. It is solely just the t-shirt with the ink surrounding the rest of the cat. So it doesn't feel blocky on the t-shirt."

**Five design principles (stated explicitly):** | **Evergreen**
1. "Simple is better"
2. "Readable from 10 feet away"
3. "One clear message"
4. "Text space works better for testing"
5. "Simple or muted colors"

**How to apply them:**
- "Keep colors one to three max"
- "Use typography and graphics"
- "Spend 5 to 10 minutes max per design"

**What makes a good design (from examples):**
- Lots of negative space — "ink is used sparingly"
- One-color or two-color designs
- Mix of typography + simple graphic ("one color, super simple. There's not a million colors all over this t-shirt")
- Funny, double-meaning concepts ("another round" — golf rounds + martini glass)
- Retro/vintage/grunge styles work ("classic retro sunset design that has been working for a long time")
- Heartbeat line designs: "I know heartbeat designs have been working for a long time"
- Cross-niche mashups: gaming + golf, animals + golf, patriotism + golf

**Patterns to look for in research:** | **Evergreen**
- Text-based designs (typography): "super easy to read, only a couple words... easy to test variations. We can simply swap out words to create new, compelling text designs"
- "Clean, readable fonts"
- "Simple concepts... just one clear message"
- "Designs with emotions or statements... Are they funny? Are they serious? Are they sad? Are they happy?"
- "Clear visual hierarchy. Is it text, design, text? Is it just design? Is it only text?"

**What makes a bad design:** | **Evergreen**
- "Straight block of ink" (no negative space)
- Color gradients: "a bunch of different color gradients, which is something we don't want to use"
- Too much text — "I feel like I'm reading a paragraph from a book. Really hard to read. Imagine standing six feet away from this design. You'd have no idea what it says."
- Pixelated / low-resolution
- Graphic bleeding into neckline
- Placement too low — "we want that graphic to be up more so it's not on someone's stomach"
- Too many colors: "super hard to read"
- AI slop look: "looks kind of like AI slop, which we're trying to avoid"
- Designs that look like "a straight meme on a t-shirt... very blocky, too much ink"

**Three mistakes never to make:** | **Evergreen**
1. "Use copyrighted images or logos"
2. "Too many colors"
3. "Design for yourself... All of your designs should be based on research"

**The translator framing:** | **Evergreen**
"You're a translator, not an artist. The market is speaking. You saw it in your research. Now you're translating that into designs."

## Scaling to 100 Designs

**Why 100 specifically:** | **Evergreen**
- "From our experience, it's 5 to 10% of designs become winners. So having 100 designs gives you 100 chances. And that's 5 to 10 potential winners. That's your foundation for $10,000 in sales."
- "100 is the magic starting point... 100 is where testing really begins. It's not the end."
- From their community survey: over 52% of sellers who hit $10K+ have 250–500 designs live. "A vast majority... have over 250 designs."
- "Top 1% of print on demand sellers make seven designs per day."

**The jar story (volume leads to quality):** | **Evergreen**
Story of college professor splitting class: group making one perfect jar vs. group making as many as possible. The volume group produced the best jar. Moral: "don't let perfect be the enemy of progress." Also: "invest the time making perfect designs after we've gotten feedback from the market, not before."

**Don't almost delete winners:** | **Evergreen**
"John almost deleted a winner. He almost deleted this by accident, but thankfully he kept it on his store. And [it] helped John scale over $2 million in sales."

**Bulk generation process (proprietary):** | **Their tool**
1. Use "We Scale Design Gem" (custom Google Gemini Gem — Gemini account required, URL shared inside their free community)
2. Split-screen: one tab for the Gem (prompts), one tab for image generation (Gemini Imagen — specifically "Nano Banana Pro" for better output)
3. Type niche name + "designs" — the Gem generates batches of prompts
4. Copy-paste each prompt into the image generation tab
5. Generate, iterate on text/layout issues via follow-up prompts
6. Use both tools in parallel: "I think ChatGPT and Gemini give vastly different styles in image generation. So something that might look good in the WeScale design GPT might not look as good in Gemini or vice versa."

**Cross-niche prompting formula:** | **Evergreen**
"[Niche] x [complementary interest]" — e.g., golf × beer, golf × skeleton, golf × gaming 8-bit pixel art, golf × rubber hose cartoon style, golf × skateboarding. "Any kind of like futuristic things... something related to the computer or something technical related to your niche where they can kind of bring both into one spot is normally the best of both worlds."

**Rubber hose / early cartoon art style note:** | **Evergreen**
"This was a, you know, early Mickey Mouse style. It's no longer copyrighted. Again, I wouldn't do anything with Mickey Mouse, but the art style is no longer copyrighted."

**Iterating on prompts (AI image generation realities):** | **Evergreen**
- "Prompting is not perfect. Same with image generation. It does take some tweaking and toying with to get exactly what we want."
- AI commonly forgets letters, misplaces text — fix with specific follow-up: "you forgot the D," "shift the [text] over to the left to fill that white space"
- "We have to describe exactly what we want the image generator to do."
- ChatGPT can remove backgrounds; Gemini cannot.

**Print-ready prep in Canva:** | **Check** (Canva-specific workflow, but principles are universal)
- Custom canvas size: 5,000 × 5,000 px for proper DPI when printing
- Canva Pro required for background removal
- After background removal: manually restore any design elements that got removed (t-shirt color, golf cart, fingers, etc.) using the "restore" brush
- Check transparent background is selected on download — ⚠️ if you skip this, the design prints with a white rectangle around it on every colored shirt. Verify by previewing on a dark shirt in Printify before publishing.
- Download as PNG zip

**File organization system:** | **Evergreen**
- One folder per design, named "[design name] t-shirt" — e.g., "Swing Hard Miss Easy t-shirt"
- Keep original AI file and upscaled/cleaned version separate
- "As you have hundreds of designs in your folder, you'll know exactly where to go for each design."

**The 100-design checklist before moving on:**
1. Generated
2. Upscaled (5,000 × 5,000)
3. Background removed
4. Named
5. Saved to folder
6. Ready to upload

**Mindset block: what slows beginners down:** | **Evergreen**
- "Don't get hung up on making each design perfect. Goal is volume first, then refinement."
- "It's not our personal taste."
- "The market will tell us what's good through the metrics on Facebook and also buying our designs." (DTC-only framing, but principle applies anywhere)
- "It will be natural for your first 10 designs to take longer. And it's completely normal. You're learning the workflow and the last 90 go so much quicker."

## Mockups That Sell

**Their core format: flatlay only.** | **DTC-only** (Explicitly optimized for Facebook feed, but flatlay principle may apply broadly)

"After 600 plus people inside the community, we have found that flatlay works the best."

Why flatlay over lifestyle:
- "We want people to picture themselves in the shirt, not seeing someone else wearing it."
- "Clean presentation style works."
- Avoid: "lifestyle mockups with models, hanger shots, elaborate staging"

**Three components of every mockup:** | **Evergreen**
1. The shirt
2. The background
3. The props

"A mockup is the background, the shirt, and any objects around it, not the design itself."

**Good mockup criteria:** | **Evergreen**
- "The t-shirt takes up as much space as possible in the image" — "t-shirt sleeves almost touch the corners"
- "Natural wrinkles in fabric"
- "Proper lighting and shadows" — "shadowing added to the t-shirt. It looks like it's actually laying on the piece of wood. There's light shining down onto it."
- "Design placement looks printed, not pasted"
- "Nothing on top of the t-shirt where our designs will go"
- "Very limited number of props"
- "Design is smooth and in the center of the chest"
- Target image size: 1,080 × 1,080 (1:1 ratio square) for Facebook feed
- "On brand" — props that match the niche (golf niche example: plaid pants, green repair pick, golf hat)

**Bad mockup criteria:** | **Evergreen**
- T-shirt floating with no shadows / looks fake
- Design off-center or too small — "when the shirt's too small, it's very hard to see the design on a t-shirt, especially in mobile"
- Too many props — "super distracting"
- Cluttered background
- Beach or irrelevant background for the niche

**The movie poster analogy:** | **Evergreen**
"A bad poster [is] just title on a colored background. Even great movies, nobody goes. Why? It doesn't communicate anything and it looks low-budget. But a good poster shows the vibe, the tone, and the quality. And you immediately know this is for me."

**Knotted t-shirt note:** | **Evergreen**
"If you were selling to women, then a knotted t-shirt does normally perform really well in feminine niches." Avoid for male-targeted stores.

**Why 10 mockup variations:** | **Evergreen**
Same logic as 100 designs — "We have more chances at finding success... different angles, different lighting, different backgrounds, different settings, and we don't know which one will work until we test it in the market."

**Proprietary mockup generation:** | **Their tool**
"We Scale Mockup Gem" (custom Google Gemini Gem, access via free community). Input: "[niche] mockup." The gem generates prompts pre-baked with winning settings (1,080×1,080, flatlay, realistic shadows, etc.). Copy-paste into separate Gemini tab (split-screen).

**Mockup iteration:** | **Check** (Gemini-specific, but principle is universal)
- Edit prompts directly when output doesn't match — e.g., remove "the bottom hem is casually knotted on one side" sentence from prompt to un-knot the shirt
- Can specify exact props: "I want a mockup for the golf niche with a golf club in it"
- Take into Canva to resize/crop so t-shirt fills the frame
- Expect roughly 4 usable out of 5 generated; some will be off-niche or off-format

**The winner mockup becomes the template:** | **Evergreen**
"That winner becomes your template for all 100 products." (Testing happens via Facebook ads — DTC-specific, but the principle of testing before committing applies anywhere.)

## Key Takeaways (3–5 bullets)

- **Research before design, always.** Spend 60 minutes on Etsy finding 10–15 proven sellers, identify 3 patterns, then stop and create. "The market is speaking. You're translating that into designs." Every design decision should trace back to something observed in the market — never personal taste.

- **Negative space is the single most important design variable.** Every winning design example they showed uses the shirt fabric as part of the design itself. One to three colors max. "Readable from 10 feet away" is the test — if you can't pass it, the design fails.

- **Volume is the strategy, not a shortcut.** "5 to 10% of designs become winners" — 100 designs = 5–10 potential winners. Start with 100, their community's $10K+ sellers average 250–500 live designs. "Invest the time making perfect designs after we've gotten feedback from the market, not before."

- **Flatlay mockups only, t-shirt filling the frame.** The mockup is the billboard for the design. Natural shadows, minimal on-brand props, 1,080×1,080 square format, nothing overlapping the design area. Make 10 variations and test — "we don't know which one will work until we test it."

- **Cross-niche formula generates infinite design directions.** "[Niche] x [complementary interest]" (golf × beer, golf × gaming, golf × patriotism) is explicitly taught as a system. Combine with audience demographics from research to prioritize which crossovers to pursue first.

## Contradictions with Video 1 (Alek)

None found in this section. The We Scale methodology reinforces evergreen principles (negative space, simple designs, volume testing) without contradicting specific claims from Alek's Video 1 instruction.

Note: We Scale is explicitly DTC/Shopify/Facebook-first. They validate designs via Facebook ad metrics ("the market will tell us through the metrics on Facebook"). This is a structural difference from an Etsy-first or organic approach — not a contradiction on design principles, but the validation loop is entirely different.
